## 1. The Core Concept of Counting in Korean

If you grew up with English, French, or Spanish, you were trained to group numbers in threes: thousands, millions, billions. Korean does not follow that rhythm. Modern Korean uses **Sino-Korean numerals** (한자어 수사) for dates, money, phone numbers, addresses, statistics, and almost every “big” number you see in the news. The mental model is not “how many millions” but **how many units of ten thousand (만)** stack on top of each other, with smaller digits (일, 십, 백, 천) filling the spaces inside each block.

This article is written for learners who already know Hangul and want a **structured, teacher-level explanation**—not a meme, not a shortcut chart. Uncle Hangul (한글아저씨) demonstrates the same logic step by step in the long-form video embedded below. Read here first, watch second, then speak the numbers aloud until your mouth remembers the pattern.

### Why 10,000 (만) is the Magic Break Point

In English, 10,000 is “ten thousand”—still inside the thousand family. In Korean, **10,000 is 만**, and it behaves like a **new comma**. Once you internalize 만, numbers such as 50,000 (오만), 120,000 (십 이만), or 9,100,000 (구백 십만) stop feeling random. Learners who ignore 만 often misplace zeros when reading prices on Naver Shopping or population figures on Yonhap News. Treat 만 as a **gear shift**: everything to the right of 만 uses 일·십·백·천; everything to the left counts **how many 만** (and later **억**, **조**) you have.

Example (price tag): **₩45,600** → 오만 사천육백 원 (literally “4-man 5,000 600 won” is wrong order—correct grouping is **4 × 10,000 + 4,000 + 600**).

Example (daily life): **만 원** is such a common cash unit that Koreans say “만 원만 주세요” when they hand a 10,000-won note.

### How This Differs From Native Korean Numbers

Korean also has **native numerals** (하나, 둘, 셋…) used for hours, age in casual speech, and counting objects under ten. Sino-Korean (일, 이, 삼…) dominates **large counts and formal contexts**. This lesson focuses on Sino-Korean because that is what you need for **910,213,090** and for Uncle Hangul’s classroom-style drills.

## 2. The Atomic Units: 일, 십, 백, 천 (1, 10, 100, 1,000)

Before 만 appears, four units repeat predictably:

**일 (il) — 1.** Standalone “one” is 일. In compounds, 1 often stays 일 (일백 = 100, not “일십일” for 11—that is 십일). Watch for **silent 일**: at the start of 100–199 in some contexts, 일 can drop in speech (백 vs 일백), but learners should read clearly at first.

**십 (sip) — 10.** Ten is always 십. Twenty is **이십** (2×10), not “스무” (native). **30 = 삼십**, **90 = 구십**. There is no irregular “-teen” series like English.

**백 (baek) — 100.** Two hundred = 이백. Six hundred = 육백. Note pronunciation: **육** can sound closer to “yuk” before a consonant.

**천 (cheon) — 1,000.** Three thousand = 삼천. When you see Western **9,000**, think **구천**, not “nine thousand” translated word-by-word.

Practice sentence (Korean / meaning):

- **이십삼** — twenty-three (23)
- **삼백사십오** — three hundred forty-five (345)
- **천구백구십** — one thousand nine hundred ninety (1,990)—common in prices referencing years or model numbers

English tip: Read Korean numbers **from largest unit to smallest within each block**, but **write them in Arabic digits left-to-right** as usual. Your eyes and mouth learn different directions.

## 3. Climbing the 만 Ladder: 십만, 백만, 천만

Once you pass 9,999 (구천구백구십구), you hit **만 (10,000)**.

**10,000 = 만**

**20,000 = 이만** (2 × 10,000)

**100,000 = 십만** (10 × 10,000)

**1,000,000 = 백만** (100 × 10,000)

**10,000,000 = 천만**

### Why Western Learners Stumble Here

An English speaker sees **1,000,000** and thinks “one million.” A Korean speaker thinks **백만**—“one hundred units of ten thousand.” Both are correct mathematically, but the **chunking** differs. When you convert mentally, **divide by 10,000 first**, not by 1,000,000.

Mini drill:

| Arabic | Korean (Sino) | English gloss |
|--------|----------------|---------------|
| 50,000 | 오만 | 5 man |
| 320,000 | 삼십 이만 | 32 man |
| 5,000,000 | 오백만 | 500 man |
| 90,000,000 | 구천만 | 9,000 man |

Say each row aloud twice: once slowly, once at conversation speed.

## 4. 억 (eok) — The Next Major Gate (100,000,000)

**억 = 100,000,000** (10⁸). It is **ten thousand man**. After mastering man, **억** is the second boss level in news Korean.

**100,000,000 = 일억** (often shortened to 억 in headlines)

**910,000,000 = 구억 천만** — notice how **구억** is the 억 slot and **천만** fills the remainder inside the next grouping.

Population and budget headlines love 억:

- **인구 구천만** — ninety million people (approx.; context clarifies exact figure)
- **예산 일조 이천억** — budgets at 조 scale (trillion / 10¹² in Korean counting—advanced, but you will see it)

For this lesson’s target number, **910,213,090** lives entirely in the **억 + man + 천** universe—no 조 required.

## 5. Worked Example: Reading 910,213,090 Aloud

Let’s map **910,213,090** the way Uncle Hangul teaches on screen:

1. Split by Korean commas conceptually: **9 | 1021 | 3090** in man-units—or think **9억 1021만 3090** with zero-padding discipline.
2. Standard reading: **구억 천이십일만 삼천구십** (9억 + 1021만 + 3090).

Step-by-step gloss for English readers:

- **구억** — 900,000,000 (9 × 1억)
- **천이십일만** — 10,210,000 (1021 × 1만)
- **삼천구십** — 3,090

Add them: 900,000,000 + 10,210,000 + 3,090 = **910,213,090**.

Common mistake: treating **213** as “two hundred thirteen thousand” English-style inside the middle chunk. Korean locks **만** after the man-slot digits. Slow practice beats cleverness.

Korean example sentences you can reuse in class or tutoring:

- **숫자 구억 천이십일만 삼천구십을 한국어로 어떻게 읽나요?** — How do you read 910,213,090 in Korean?
- **큰 숫자는 만 단위로 끊어 읽으면 쉬워요.** — Large numbers are easier when you cut at man units.

## 6. Classroom Tips

Numbers improve when you combine **eyes, mouth, and hand**:

Write the Arabic numeral, draw vertical bars at 만 and 억 boundaries, then write the Korean reading underneath. Do not copy-paste from Google Translate audio until you have attempted the reading yourself—Translate is useful for checking, not for first contact.

When studying with the video **crpmZ-bjcsU**, pause every time a new unit (십, 백, 천, 만, 십만, 백만, 천만, 억) appears on screen. Recite the unit name in Korean, then in English, then combine. This **bilingual anchor** prevents the “I recognize the shape but freeze when speaking” problem.

If your goal is AdSense-level literacy for living in Korea—reading apartment prices, phone contracts, or vaccination statistics—you do not need every native counter. You **do** need Sino-Korean fluency at the **만/억** level. This single skill transfers to dates (년월일), time ranges in formal documents, and bank SMS alerts.
