## Introduction: Foreign Words in Korea Are Not New

If you take a quiet walk through the streets of Seoul today, the visual landscape is filled with familiar global terms rendered in Hangul script: 커피 (Coffee), 피자 (Pizza), 파스타 (Pasta). It is easy to view this constant transliteration as a purely modern phenomenon—a byproduct of 21st-century globalization and digital connectivity.

However, the Korean Peninsula has always sat at a dynamic geopolitical crossroads. For centuries, its geography made it a natural converging point for diverse cultures, trade routes, and diplomatic networks.

Continental neighbors like China, Mongolia, and the Jurchen peoples interacted continuously along the northern borders, while maritime channels connected the peninsula to Japan. Silk Road merchants from as far as Central Asia and the Arab world brought their distinct spoken traditions to Korean trading ports long before the modern era.

Long before the creation of Hangul, the peninsula vibrated with a rich tapestry of foreign speech. When a native writing system was finally developed in the 15th century, it was designed from its inception with a structural framework capable of receiving and rendering these varied sounds with remarkable visual clarity.

## King Sejong’s Vision and Research Beyond Borders

When King Sejong initiated the creation of Hunminjeongeum (훈민정음) in the 1440s, his objective reached beyond providing a functional writing system for his own people. As a statesman navigating complex regional affairs, he recognized that effective diplomacy and trade required an exact, nuanced understanding of foreign speech.

At the time, written communication across East Asia relied on Classical Chinese characters (한문). While Chinese characters served as a shared literary medium, they were ideographic rather than phonetic; they conveyed meaning well, but failed to capture how people actually spoke across different regions and dialects.

To address this gap, King Sejong directed scholars from the Hall of Worthies (집현전)—including Shin Suk-ju (신숙주) and Seong Sam-mun (성삼문)—to conduct empirical phonetic research. Scholars were dispatched on multiple trips to border regions and diplomatic outposts to consult with foreign speech experts and Chinese phonologists.

They analyzed throat shapes, tongue positions, and subtle variations in pitch and breath, seeking to build a systematic alphabet that could visually mirror spoken sound across borders.

## The Sayeokwon (司譯院) and Diplomatic Textbooks

Following the introduction of Hangul, this research was put into immediate practical application at the Sayeokwon (사역원), the official Joseon government agency responsible for foreign language education and diplomatic translation.

To train diplomats and official interpreters, the Sayeokwon published a series of comprehensive language textbooks. Instead of teaching abstract grammatical theory, these books focused on practical, spoken dialogue, accompanied by precise Hangul annotations detailing exact pronunciations:

> **Chinese: Nogeoldae (노걸대)**
> A practical conversational guide for merchants traveling along trade routes, featuring everyday dialogues regarding commerce, lodging, and travel.

> **Japanese: Cheophae Sin-eo (첩해신어)**
> A diplomatic textbook designed for Korean officials interacting with Japanese envoys, documenting contemporary spoken Japanese phrasing.

> **Mongolian & Manchu: Mong-eo Nogeoldae (몽어노걸대) and Cheong-eo Nogeoldae (청어노걸대)**
> Adaptations of the core conversational curriculum tailored specifically for northern continental relations.

The defining feature of these Sayeokwon texts was their dual-layer annotation system. The original foreign text was printed alongside Hangul characters mapped directly to the local spoken sounds of the era, capturing subtle tone shifts, regional accents, and colloquial phrasing that traditional character scripts could not register.

## How Hangul Preserved Historical Phonetics

Languages evolve continuously over time. Vowels shift, consonants merge, and regional accents transform. In many cases, the exact spoken sounds of 15th- to 18th-century East Asia vanished from living memory, leaving behind written Chinese texts that offer few clues about historical pronunciation.

Because the Joseon Dynasty maintained a rigorous tradition of historical documentation, the Sayeokwon’s Hangul-annotated textbooks survived intact. By capturing regional speech using Hangul's modular, sound-based alphabet, these materials inadvertently created a detailed phonetic record of Early Modern East Asian languages.

Today, international linguists—from Japan, China, Mongolia, and beyond—frequently consult these historic Joseon texts. When researchers seek to reconstruct how spoken Mandarin sounded during the Ming Dynasty, or how Japanese was articulated in the Edo period, the Sayeokwon manuscripts provide a clear, empirical reference point preserved through time.

## Personal Reflection: Viewing Hangul Through Sound

Examining these 15th-century diplomatic manuscripts offers a perspective on Hangul that goes beyond standard language learning. It highlights a writing system designed around the visual mapping of acoustic reality.

From a design perspective, the appeal of Hangul lies in its geometric simplicity. Sounds are not hidden behind arbitrary spelling rules or layered grammar conventions; instead, spoken input maps directly into clean visual frames. The shape of a character reflects the physical space where a sound is formed in the mouth and throat.

Viewing Hangul through this lens allows learners to appreciate it not as a list of grammar rules to be memorized, but as a thoughtfully structured design system—one built centuries ago to bring clarity and visual form to human speech.
