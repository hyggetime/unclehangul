## Why Koreans Ask Your Zodiac Animal, Not Just Your Age

**If you spend time around Koreans**, you will quickly notice that direct questions about age can feel slightly blunt. Instead of asking "How old are you?", people often ask "What year were you born?" or "What is your zodiac animal?" (**무슨 띠예요?**).

**This question serves a practical social purpose.** In Korea, social hierarchy and speech levels (**존댓말** and **반말**) depend heavily on age relative to others. Knowing your zodiac animal allows Koreans to establish social standing instantly without making anyone feel singled out about their exact birth year.

**Your zodiac animal**, known as **띠** (*tti*), is rooted in the 12 Earthly Branches (**십이지신**), an ancient East Asian lunar cycle. Every year in the 12-year cycle belongs to a specific guardian animal, repeating sequentially from the Rat to the Pig.

**In Korean culture**, your connection to your birth animal begins even before you are born. Parents pay close attention to **태몽** (*taemong*), or conception dreams, where animals like pigs, dragons, or tigers appear as prophetic symbols for the child's future. If you want to explore how parents interpret these dreams before a child arrives, read our deep dive on the [Korean Taemong Experience](/learn/tae-mong-birth-dream-experience-korea).

## How to Say Your Zodiac Animal in Korean

**Expressing your zodiac animal in Korean** requires one simple sentence structure. You do not need complex grammar to state your **띠**.

**The sentence structure breaks down as follows:**

* **저** (*jeo*) = I (Polite form)
* **는** (*neun*) = Topic marker
* **[Animal] + 띠** (*tti*) = Zodiac animal designation
* **예요** / **이에요** (*yeo-yo* / *i-e-yo*) = Am / Is / Are (Polite ending)

**Because all 12 animal names combined with 띠 end in a vowel sound**, you will consistently use **예요** at the end of the sentence.

For example, if you were born in the year of the Dragon (**용**):

> **저는 용띠예요.**
> (*Jeo-neun yong-tti-yeo-yo.*)
> "I am a Dragon."

## What Does Your Animal Say About You?

**In traditional Korean folklore**, each animal of the **십이지신** carries distinct personality traits and cultural expectations:

* **Tiger (호랑이)**: Associated with bravery, leadership, and protection against bad fortune.
* **Rabbit (토끼)**: Linked with intelligence, gentleness, and quick thinking.
* **Pig (돼지)**: Historically tied to wealth, financial abundance, and good fortune.

**Artists across centuries** documented these guardian animals in royal screens and folk art to protect homes and mark time.
