**If learning Korean numbers feels like taking a pop math quiz in another language**, you are not alone. Korean uses two completely separate number systems—Native Korean and Sino-Korean—and figuring out which one to use can give anyone an instant headache.

Here is the simple mental model to remember: **Native Korean** (*Hana, Dul, Set...*) is for things you see and point at with your eyes (physical items, ages, and clock hours), while **Sino-Korean** (*Il, I, Sam...*) is for data on a screen or calculator (money, dates, phone numbers, and clock minutes).

---

## Situation 1: Native Korean Numbers – Counting Drinks at a Cafe

**When you are pointing at physical items or ordering drinks**, rely on Native Korean numbers. Keep in mind that numbers like *Hana* (1), *Dul* (2), and *Set* (3) shorten slightly to *Han*, *Du*, and *Se* right before a counter word like *Jan* (cup).

### Dialogue Example (Ordering drinks at a cafe)

* **Korean:** **아메리카노 두 잔 주세요.**
* **Pronunciation:** [Ah-me-ri-ka-no du jan ju-se-yo.]
* **Literal Translation:** Americano two cups give please.
* **Natural English:** "Two Americanos, please."
* **Vocabulary Breakdown:** **아메리카노** (Americano) | **두** (Two — Native form before a counter) | **잔** (Glass / Cup counter) | **주세요** (Please give)

---

## Situation 2: Sino-Korean Numbers – Paying Money at Checkout

**Prices and money are calculated numbers** that you read off a screen or receipt. Because money is pure mathematical data, always use Sino-Korean numbers (*Il*, *I*, *Sam*, *Sa*...).

### Dialogue Example (Paying at a store)

* **Korean:** **구천원이에요.**
* **Pronunciation:** [Gu-cheon-won-i-e-yo.]
* **Literal Translation:** 9,000 Won it is.
* **Natural English:** "That will be 9,000 Won."
* **Vocabulary Breakdown:** **구천** (9,000 — Sino) | **원** (Won — Korean currency) | **이에요** (It is)

---

## Situation 3: The Hybrid Rule – Telling Time (2:30)

**Telling time is the ultimate test** that confuses new learners because it combines both systems into a single sentence. Hours use Native numbers (think of pointing at the 12 visible numbers on a physical clock face), while minutes use Sino numbers (because 1 to 60 is precise math data).

### Dialogue Example (Setting a meetup time)

* **Korean:** **우리 두 시 삼십 분에 봐요!**
* **Pronunciation:** [U-ri du si sam-sip bun-e bwa-yo!]
* **Literal Translation:** We two o'clock thirty minute at see!
* **Natural English:** "Let's meet at 2:30!"
* **Vocabulary Breakdown:** **우리** (We) | **두** (2 — Native for hours) | **시** (O'clock) | **삼십** (30 — Sino for minutes) | **분** (Minute) | **에** (At) | **봐요** (See / Meet)

---

## Uncle Hangul's Quick Recap

* **하나, 둘, 셋** (Native Korean): Use for physical things you can count or point at (cups, items, age) + Hours (**시**).
* **일, 이, 삼** (Sino-Korean): Use for calculated data on a screen (money, prices, phone numbers) + Minutes (**분**).
* **Pro Tip for 2:30:** Remember *"Du-si (Native) Sam-sip-bun (Sino)"*—hours are intuitive, while minutes are mathematical!

**Once you connect Native numbers to visual items and Sino numbers to screen data**, switching between the two becomes second nature.
